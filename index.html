A teen media-literacy project

Teen life is more than a movie scene.

Movies can help us feel understood, but they can also make certain ideas about popularity, appearance and growing up seem normal. Explore how teenage characters are shown on screen — and remember that real life is more complicated than a script.

01 / The film shelf

Five films. Different versions of being a teenager.

Select “Explore the themes” on each card to see what the film can help us think about. These are interpretations, not claims that every teenager experiences the same things.

Mean Girls

2004 · Comedy

Cady enters a new school and learns how social groups, rumours and status can shape the way people treat one another.

PopularitySocial status
Think about:

How do characters change their behaviour to gain approval? Does the film challenge popularity rules, or sometimes make them look appealing?

Discussion question:

How can labels like “popular” or “outcast” affect how someone sees themselves?

The Breakfast Club

1985 · Drama

Five students from different school groups spend a day in detention and discover that their labels do not explain their whole lives.

StereotypesIdentity
Think about:

The characters are introduced through familiar school stereotypes, then reveal more complicated feelings and experiences.

Discussion question:

Why is it unfair to assume you know someone based on one label?

The Edge of Seventeen

2016 · Coming-of-age

Nadine deals with friendship changes, family tension and the feeling that everyone else has life figured out.

Self-imageBelonging
Think about:

The story presents awkwardness and emotional reactions as part of growing up, rather than making the main character perfect.

Discussion question:

How might seeing an imperfect character make viewers feel less alone?

Thirteen

2003 · Drama

Tracy's changing friendships and choices show the pressure to be accepted and the risks of seeking approval.

Peer pressureChoices
Think about:

The film explores how the need to belong can influence decisions. Its intense story is one portrayal, not a typical experience for every teen.

Discussion question:

What can make it difficult to say no when a group is pressuring someone?

Clueless

1995 · Comedy

Cher navigates school, friendship, style and relationships while learning that her first impressions are not always right.

AppearanceFirst impressions
Think about:

Fashion and popularity are a big part of the film's world, but Cher also has room to learn and reconsider her assumptions.

Discussion question:

How can a film make appearance seem important, and how can viewers question that message?

02 / Patterns to notice

What messages do teen movies send?

Use these prompts to look beyond the plot and think about how a story represents teenage life.

Appearance and beauty standards

Costumes, styling, camera angles and comments about looks can suggest which appearances are admired. Ask whether different body types, styles and identities are represented fairly.

Popularity and social status

Stories may show popularity as power, safety or success. Notice whether characters are valued for their personality or mainly for their social position.

Stereotypes and labels

Characters can be simplified into roles such as “the brain,” “the athlete” or “the popular one.” Think about what gets lost when a person is reduced to one label.

Peer pressure and belonging

Teen characters sometimes change their choices or appearance to fit in. Ask what the character wants, what the group expects, and what the consequences are.

03 / Interactive reality check

A movie message is not a rule for real life.

Read each statement and reveal a question that can help you think about it critically.

“Everyone at school cares about popularity.”
A film may focus on social status because it creates conflict. In real life, people care about different things, and school communities are not all the same. Ask: whose perspective does the story leave out?
“You have to look a certain way to be liked.”
Movies use styling and visuals to communicate quickly, but appearance does not determine a person's worth. Ask: is the film questioning beauty standards, repeating them, or doing both?
“Teenagers are always dramatic and reckless.”
Conflict makes stories interesting, so films may focus on intense moments. That does not mean every teenager behaves this way. Ask: is this one character being treated as if they represent everyone?
“Changing yourself is the only way to fit in.”
Some stories show characters changing to gain approval, but belonging can also come from friendships where people accept differences. Ask: what does the character gain, and what might they lose?
